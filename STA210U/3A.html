1. Simple Probability

Sample Space - the set of all possible outcomes from an experiment

Example

Identify the sample space for each of the following

  • Roll a die
\[\text{sample space}=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\]
  • Flip a coin
\[\text{sample space}=\{H,T\}\]

The above example illustrates a common way to denote a sample space. Notice that when you roll a die you get a discrete quantitative variable and when you flip a coin you get a qualitative variable. These are the types of variables we will deal with to introduce the basic ideas of probability.

Event - a subset of the sample space

Example

Suppose you roll a die and event A is defined by getting an even number. We would represent this event as:

\[A=\{2,4,6\}\]
Probability (of an event A) - denoted \(P(A)\), is the expected proportion of occurrences of A if the experiment were performed a large number of times (keep in mind A is just an example, we could call the event whatever we wanted)
\[\text{Probability of an event}=\frac{\#\text{ of outcomes in the event}}{\text{Total \# of outcomes}}\]

We will look at a couple counting principal problems in order to understand how to identify the number of outcomes when there are a series of operations in an experiment.

2. Common Probabilities

Complement - (of an event A) denoted \(P(\bar A)\), is all sample points not in A
Joint Probability - (A or B) denoted \(P(A\text{ or }B)\) or (A and B) denoted \(P(A\text{ and }B)\), is an event that has two or more characteristics
Conditional Probability - (A conditioned on B) denoted \(P(A\mid B)\), is when we are interested in knowing if event A occurred given that we know event B occurred

3. Probability Rules

Complement rule

\[P(\bar A)=1-P(A)\]

Addition rule

\[P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)\]

Conditional probability rule

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}\]

Multiplication rule

\[P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\]

4. Characteristics of Events

Mutually Exclusive or Disjoint - when two events cannot occur together
\[\text{If }P(A\text{ and }B)=0\text{ then A and B are mutually exclusive}\]

You should notice that when you have mutually exclusive events the last term of the addition rule, \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)\), is zero.

Independence

Independent - two events are said to be independent if the occurrence (or nonoccurrence) of one does not affect the probability of occurrence of the other
\[\text{If }P(A)=P(A\mid B)\text{ then A and B are independent.}\]

Thinking about this relationship, we see that when given B, the probability of A has not changed from its original value. Or in other words, B occurring does not affect the probability of A.

Dependent - events that are not independent
\[\text{If }P(A)\ne P(A\mid B)\text{ then A and B are dependent}\]

Example

Suppose we are dealing with a standard deck of cards (52 cards and 4 aces). We will draw two cards without replacement (meaning after the first card is drawn, it will not be put back in the deck before drawing the second card).

\(A=\{\text{first card is an ace}\}\)   \(B=\{\text{second card is an ace}\}\)

  1. In this scenario are A and B dependent or independent?
  2. Find the \(P(A\text{ and }B)\)

Suppose we change the scenario and return the first card, thoroughly shuffle the deck, and then draw the second card. (draw with replacement)

  1. With this change are A and B dependent or independent?
  2. Find the \(P(A\text{ and }B)\)
Show Answers

1. A and B are dependent. If the first card is an ace then the probability the second card is an ace is 3/51 but if the first card is not an ace then the probability the second card is an ace is 4/51. The probability of B depends on A.

2. Multiplication rule \(P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)=\left(\frac{4}{52}\right)\left(\frac{3}{51}\right)=.004525\)

3. A and B are independent. No matter what happens with the first draw, the probability the second card is an ace is 4/52. The probability of B does not depend on A.

4. \(P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)=\left(\frac{4}{52}\right)\left(\frac{4}{52}\right)=.00591\)

5. Misconceptions of Probability

Gambler’s fallacy - thinking that the long-run frequency of an event should apply to the short run

Some people believe that when gambling, a string of bad luck will be followed by good luck. Is this true? No, because of independence the probability of more bad luck does not change.

Probability of ‘Very Unlikely’ Events

As long as we agree on the physical assumptions underlying an uncertain process, we should agree on the probability of outcomes. In the simplest of cases if we flip a coin and agree the outcomes are equally likely, then we can agree that the probability of getting tails is 0.5. Every probability is going to be calculated based on some assumption.

Most people think that coincidences have low probabilities of occurring. Is this correct?

An event may be exceedingly improbable but what is not improbable is that someone, somewhere, someday will experience those events or something similar.

Example

In a four month period of time a lady in New Jersey won the state lottery twice (getting millions of dollars each time). The local newspaper reported that the chance of this occurring is 1 in 17 trillion. When reading such a probability, you should always ask yourself what assumptions were made. It is true that the probability of a specific individual who plays the New Jersey state lottery exactly twice wins both times is 1 in 17 trillion. However, millions of people play the lottery every day, and it is not surprising that someone, somewhere, someday would win twice. In fact, if you take all possible opportunities for a double winner in a 4-month period of time, there is a 1 in 30 chance of this phenomena occurring.

Example

What do you think the chances are that two people in a class of 32 students have the same birthday? Let us start with only four people so you can see how the math works. We will make two assumptions that are not exactly true. We will assume nobody was born on February 29th and also that all birthdays are equally likely.

What is the probability that four people have different birthdays? (Multiplication rule)

\[\frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdot\frac{363}{365}\cdot\frac{362}{365} =\frac{17,458,601,160}{17,748,900,625}=0.983\]

What is the probability that at least two out of four people have the same birthday? (Complement rule)

\[1-0.983=.017\]

Now do the same thing for 32 people and you will find that the probability that everyone in the class has a different birthday is 0.255. This means that the probability at least two people in this class have the same birthday is 0.745 or 74.5%. Is this what you expected? The more people you include, the more of an opportunity there is for the event to occur. Thus, the higher the probability will be.
