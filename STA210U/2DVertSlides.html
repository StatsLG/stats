What Is \(r\)?

Pearson correlation, \(r\), describes the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables.
It only measures the linear relationship.

The Range of \(r\)

\[-1\le r\le1\]

\(r=1\)

Perfect positive linear correlation

\(r=-1\)

Perfect negative linear correlation

\(r=0\)

No linear relationship

Course Strength Rule

\(|r|\ge .75\)

In this course, a relationship is generally considered strong when the absolute value of \(r\) is at least .75.

The sign tells direction. The absolute value tells strength.

Correlation ≠ Causation

A relationship between two variables does not, by itself, prove that one causes the other.
Causal research aims to determine whether one variable causes changes in another.

Causation Criteria: 1 & 2

1. Time order

The cause must occur before the effect.

2. Co-variation

The graph and correlation coefficient must show a strong relationship.

Linear Correlation & Regression
STA210U • Section 2D

Causation Criteria: 3 & 4

3. Rationale

There must be a logical and compelling explanation for the causal mechanism.

4. Non-spuriousness

Rival explanations must be ruled out.

The fourth criterion is especially difficult.

What Is Regression?

Regression — a technique used to predict variables based on other variables.
Linear regression predicts Y from X using a linear equation.

Why the Scatterplot Matters

One extreme point can pull the least-squares line toward itself and greatly increase \(|r|\).

Never trust \(r\) alone. Always inspect the scatterplot for unusual or influential observations.

Big Example 2: Temperature & Gas Bill

Question: Is average outdoor temperature linearly related to a household's monthly natural-gas bill?

Before revealing anything: predict the direction of the relationship.
Linear Correlation & Regression
STA210U • Section 2D

Step 1: Correlation

What do the scatterplot and \(r\) say?

\(r\approx-0.967\)

Strong negative linear relationship.

Warmer months tend to have lower natural-gas bills.

Linear Correlation & Regression
STA210U • Section 2D

Step 2: \(R^2\)

How much variation in gas bills does the linear model explain?

93.4%

About 93.4% of the variation in monthly gas bills is explained by the linear relationship with average temperature in these observations.

Linear Correlation & Regression
STA210U • Section 2D

Step 3: Regression

\[\hat Y\approx285.48-2.82X\]

For each additional 1°F, the model predicts the monthly gas bill will be about $2.82 lower, on average.

Linear Correlation & Regression
STA210U • Section 2D

Step 4: Predict

Predict the bill at 38°F.

\[\hat Y=285.48-2.82(38)\]
≈ $178

38°F is inside the observed 22–76°F range, so this is interpolation.

STA210U • Section 2D

Step 5: Be Careful

What about 10°F?

The equation predicts about $257.

But 10°F is outside the data range → extrapolation.

Not from correlation alone. Bills can also depend on home size, insulation, thermostat settings, furnace efficiency, occupancy, and gas prices.
