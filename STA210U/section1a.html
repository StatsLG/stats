1. Basic Statistical Terminology

How do you define statistics?

What do you think of when you hear the term statistics? Can you think of examples where you see statistics utilized?

Statistics are commonly used in most fields of study and are regularly seen in newspapers, on television, and in professional work.

Finding examples pertinent to your interests should be quite easy. Below are a few of the many possible examples:

Examples of Statistics

Sports

  • Free throw percentage in basketball
  • Quarterback rating in football
  • RBIs in baseball
  • Assists in soccer

Medical

  • The success rates and likelihood of side effects with a weight-loss drug
  • The percentage of people who go into remission with a type of cancer
  • The effect of a drug on blood pressure

Education

  • Writing down material helps in comprehension
  • Reading test scores in 1st graders are different for males and females
  • Depression is more likely in a student with low academic achievement

Politics

  • Prediction that the incumbent president will be re-elected
  • Approval rating of the Senate
  • Percentage of people in Kentucky who support a bill that just passed

Definition of Statistics

Statistics — the study of how to collect, organize, analyze, and interpret information.

The advantage of statistics is that it gives a process for estimation and decision making when faced with uncertainties without prejudice (bias).

This ideal will be discussed throughout the material this semester.

In order to understand the statistical process, we will begin with some basic statistical terminology.

Population

Population — the collection of individuals or items of interest.

Examples:

  1. All residents of the United States
  2. All admits to hospitals in Lexington, Kentucky

Census

Census — measurements from the entire population are used.

When do you see a census utilized?

A common response is the census conducted every 10 years by the U.S. Census Bureau.

  • They attempt to reach every resident in the United States.
    • Do you think this is possible? What problems do you think they face?
    • One of the biggest problems is reaching the homeless population. It is difficult to contact or even know how many individuals are in this group.
    • Unlike most organizations, the Census Bureau has legal authority that obligates people to complete the census. Therefore, they come very close to having a true census.

Often, it is not feasible to study the entire population. The main reasons include:

  • A census is rarely attempted because of the cost. It is very expensive to conduct a census unless you are dealing with a very small population.
  • In statistical practice, it is usually impossible to get everyone, or even close to everyone, to take part in a study. Participants must agree to take part, and obviously not everyone will agree.

So, if we cannot take measurements from the entire population, what should we do?

We often take measurements from a subset, or smaller group of individuals, instead of from the entire population.

Important Terminology

Sample — the subset of the population on which we make measurements.
Data — the measurements we collect.
Individuals — the objects described by a set of data. Individuals can be people, animals, or things.
Variable — any characteristic of an individual that can take different values for different individuals.
Statistic — a numerical characteristic of a sample.

This value is known when we take our sample, but it will change from sample to sample.

Parameter — a numerical characteristic of a population.

This value is a fixed number, but we could only know its value if we conducted a perfect census.

Since the population is often not available, we use statistics to estimate parameters.

Example

Suppose we are interested in estimating the average income for all residents of the state of Kentucky. We select 1,000 people from the state and collect their incomes.

2. Branches of Statistics

We are now ready to look at the two main parts, or branches, in the science of statistics.

Descriptive Statistics — methods of summarizing a set of data.

There is no error here because you are summarizing the data you actually have.

Inferential Statistics — methods of making inferences about a population based on information contained in a sample.

There is error here because you do not have all of your data. You have only a subset of the population and cannot expect to be exactly correct.

We can control and calculate this error using probability. Therefore, probability will be covered before inferential statistics.

Examples

Identify each of the following as descriptive or inferential statistics.

  1. A basketball player's free throw percentage based on performance up to the midpoint of the season.
  2. The average GPA for all current students based on complete data from the records office.
  3. The president's current approval rating based on a group of 5,000 U.S. residents.
  4. A graph showing all sales this year for all major vehicle manufacturers.
  5. Suppose I collect data from this class and calculate the percentage of students from the class who live in Lexington.
  6. Suppose I collect data from this class and use it to estimate the percentage of all BCTC students who live in Lexington.
Show Answers
  1. Descriptive — there is no error since we know exactly how many free throws the player has attempted and made at this point in the season.
  2. Descriptive — there is no error since we have the GPA of all current students.
  3. Inferential — we do not have data from the entire group of interest. A sample of 5,000 U.S. residents is being used to estimate the approval rating for all U.S. residents.
  4. Descriptive — all sales figures are being summarized. Almost all graphs and charts are descriptive.
  5. Descriptive — we have data from the entire group of interest: this class.
  6. Inferential — students in this class are being used as a sample to estimate a characteristic of all BCTC students.

Notice that examples 5 and 6 use the same data. However, the question is different, which causes one analysis to be descriptive and the other to be inferential.

The group of interest is the key to identifying whether there will be error.

3. Types of Studies

Study — a method of collecting and processing data.

There are three common types of studies introduced in this section.

Observational Study

Observational Study — observes individuals and measures variables of interest but does not attempt to influence responses.

Simulation

Simulation — a numerical facsimile of real-world phenomena.

Simulations can fit real-world problems extremely well and can be used to study situations that might otherwise be dangerous to human life.

They are not normally used unless necessary because they can be very expensive in terms of human labor and computer resources.

Experiment

Experiment — deliberately imposes some treatment on individuals in order to observe their responses.

The purpose of an experiment is to study whether the treatment causes a change in the response.

Terminology Used When Conducting an Experiment

Subjects — the individuals studied in an experiment.
Treatment — one or a combination of treatment variables assigned by the experimenter.
Treatment Diagram — a table useful in determining all treatment combinations.

Experiment Example

Suppose we are conducting an experiment on weight loss and are interested in testing the effect of:

  • Diet: Fat-free and Atkins
  • Exercise: Swimming, walking, and cycling

To determine the number of treatment combinations, multiply the number of categories from each treatment variable:

2 diets × 3 exercises = 6 treatment combinations

Exercise Fat-Free Atkins
Swimming 1 2
Walking 3 4
Cycling 5 6

When conducting a study, subjects must be included in all treatment combinations in order to run statistical tests on each treatment variable.

A test can also be conducted to examine the relationship among the treatment variables.

Identify the Type of Study

  1. In a medical study, patients are given a drug at various dosage levels to study effectiveness.
  2. Various vehicles are studied to see which are safest in a head-on collision.
  3. The outcome of an upcoming presidential election is predicted by asking individuals over the telephone who they plan to vote for.
  4. Change a container from Pepsi to Coke and calculate the percentage of people who notice the change.
Show Answers
  1. Experiment — the drug is imposed and the purpose is to measure its effect at different dosages.
  2. Simulation — crash tests commonly use simulations because conducting the experiment with people inside the vehicles would obviously be unsafe.
  3. Observational Study — this is a survey where we are not attempting to influence responses.
  4. Experiment — the change from Pepsi to Coke is imposed and researchers measure whether people notice the change.

4. Types of Data

Qualitative (Categorical) Variable — places responses into categories and there is no numerical meaning to the outcomes.
Quantitative Variable — numeric values that have numerical meaning.
  • Discrete Variable — things that can be counted.
  • Continuous Variable — things that are measured.

Examples

Identify the data type for each variable.

  1. Gender
  2. Time to run a mile
  3. Number of soccer goals scored
  4. Political preference
  5. Distance to drive to work
  6. Number of children
  7. Social Security number
Show Answers
  1. Qualitative
  2. Quantitative — Continuous
  3. Quantitative — Discrete
  4. Qualitative
  5. Quantitative — Continuous
  6. Quantitative — Discrete
  7. Qualitative

5. Levels of Measurement

Nominal Level — identifies an individual or a characteristic. These quantities have no implied criteria or ordering.
Ordinal Level — like nominal, with the addition that values can be arranged in some order. Ordinal data provide relative comparisons, but actual differences between values either cannot be determined or are meaningless.
Interval Level — like ordinal, with the addition of meaningful differences between data values. Interval data do not have an intrinsic zero or starting point. Therefore, differences are meaningful, but ratios are not.
Ratio Level — similar to interval, with the addition of an intrinsic zero. This makes both differences and ratios meaningful.
