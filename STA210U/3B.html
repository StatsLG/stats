1. Dichotomous Tests

Dichotomous Test - a test with only two possible results

The concept of a dichotomous test is important in the field of statistics. Common examples of these tests include: roadside sobriety test (drunk or not drunk), home pregnancy test (pregnant or not pregnant), and cancer screening (have cancer or do not have cancer). The importance of these tests in the field of statistics extends to the idea of a statistical test (hypothesis test). Later in the course, we will cover hypothesis testing when dealing with inferential statistics. In hypothesis testing, we will have two possible outcomes and will use probability to deal with the error in the tests. In many respects, hypothesis testing is similar to the dichotomous tests introduced here. For example, we may collect data to test and see if a new drug is effective in treating a type of illness. Based on the results, either we will conclude that the drug is effective or there will not be enough evidence to conclude the drug is effective. For now, we will focus on the probability involved in a dichotomous test.

Dichotomous test are summarized based on the four conditional probabilities defined below.

False Positive Rate (FPR) - the probability a dichotomous test concludes positive given the actual condition is negative (in other words, the rate of positive test results in subjects who do not have the attribute tested)
\[\text{False Positive Rate}=P(\text{test concludes positive}\mid\text{actual condition is negative})\]
\[=\frac{\text{Number of subjects who get positive test results but do not have the attribute tested}}{\text{Number of subjects who do not have the attribute tested}}\]
Specificity - the probability a dichotomous test concludes negative given the actual condition is negative (in other words, the rate of negative test results in subjects who do not have the attribute tested)

Notice that FPR + Specificity = 1 because these two rates include all the possible outcomes for subjects who do not have the attribute tested. Specificity is the complement of a false positive rate.

\[\text{Specificity}=1-\text{False Positive Rate}\]
\[=P(\text{test concludes negative}\mid\text{actual condition is negative})\]
\[=\frac{\text{Number of subjects who get negative test results and do not have the attribute tested}}{\text{Number of subjects who do not have the attribute tested}}\]
False Negative Rate (FNR) - the probability a dichotomous test concludes negative given the actual condition is positive (in other words, the rate of negative test results in subjects who do have the attribute tested)
\[\text{False Negative Rate}=P(\text{test concludes negative}\mid\text{actual condition is positive})\]
\[=\frac{\text{Number of subjects who get negative test results but do not have the attribute tested}}{\text{Number of subjects who do have the attribute tested}}\]
Sensitivity - the probability a dichotomous test concludes positive given the actual condition is positive (in other words, the rate of positive test results in subjects who do have the attribute tested)

Notice that FNR + Sensitivity = 1 because these two rates include all the possible outcomes for subjects who do have the attribute tested. Sensitivity is the complement of a false negative rate.

\[\text{Sensitivity}=1-\text{False Negative Rate}\]
\[=P(\text{test concludes positive}\mid\text{actual condition is positive})\]
\[=\frac{\text{Number of subjects who get positive test results and do have the attribute tested}}{\text{Number of subjects who do have the attribute tested}}\]
Notice that the denominator in each of the fractions above is the actual condition of the subject.
  • To calculate the false positive rate or the specificity, the denominator is the number of subjects who do not have the attribute tested.
  • To calculate the false negative rate or the sensitivity, the denominator is the number of subjects who do have the attribute tested.

The false positive rate and the false negative rate represent the probability of the test being incorrect (probability of error). For a good test, these values will be small, indicating a small amount of error in the result. Conversely, the specificity and sensitivity identify the likelihood of the test being correct (complements of the errors). These values will be high when there is a small amount of error in the test. It is not necessary to look at all four probabilities when determining if a test is good. Commonly these types of dichotomous tests only report the sensitivity and specificity of the test.

2. Valid Discrete Probability Distributions

First, it is important to make sure that we distinguish between a discrete and continuous random variable. While the idea of a probability distribution is the same in both cases, the way we have to look at the probability distribution is different. We will focus on the idea of probability for a discrete random variable here.

Discrete Random Variable - potential values are separated points on the number line; there is a finite or countable number of values (outcomes are typically counted)
Continuous Random Variable - potential values fall in an interval on the number line; there is an infinite number of values (outcomes are typically measured)

A discrete random variable will include a finite or countable number of outcomes. Therefore, we assign probabilities to each value of the random variable.

Probability distribution - the collection of \(P(x)\) values for all possible \(x\) values

For a discrete probability distribution to be valid, it must satisfy the following:

  1. The variable, \(x\), must be discrete
  2. \(0\le P(x)\le1\) for all \(x\)
  3. \(\displaystyle\sum P(x)=1\)

3. Mean of a Discrete Probability Distribution (Expected Value)

Expected Value - the mean of a probability distribution (This value represents the same quantity as the mean did in section II. B. The difference in the calculation comes from the fact that outcomes in a discrete probability distribution are not equally likely in most cases. Thus, this calculation is a weighted average. If all probabilities are the same then the calculation from section II. B. will give the same outcome. The other big difference here as compared to what we looked at in section II. B. is that we are now dealing with a population mean and not a sample mean. In a probability distribution, we have all of the possible outcomes meaning that we are describing the population.)

The population mean for a discrete probability distribution is:

\[\mu=\sum xP(x)\]

\(\mu\) is a lowercase Greek letter said as ‘mu’ (Can be any real number)

The difference in notation for the mean and standard deviation as compared to section II.B. is important. We are now dealing with population values. It is crucial to remember the notation for both the sample and population because later in the course you will see population and sample values in the same formula.

Show Answer
\[\mu=\sum xP(x)=1\left(\frac16\right)+2\left(\frac16\right)+3\left(\frac16\right)+4\left(\frac16\right)+5\left(\frac16\right)+6\left(\frac16\right)=3.5\]

4. Standard Deviation of a Discrete Probability Distribution

As always in order to calculate standard deviation, we must start by calculating the variance. The population variance for a discrete probability distribution is:

\[\sigma^2=\sum (x-\mu)^2P(x)=\sum x^2P(x)-\mu^2\]

The population standard deviation is the square root of the variance.

\[\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\]

\(\sigma\) is a lowercase Greek letter said as ‘sigma’ (Can be any nonnegative real number)

Example 3 (video solution)

Roll a die and \(x\) is the number rolled. Find the standard deviation of \(x\).

Show Answer
\[\sigma^2=\sum x^2P(x)-\mu^2=\frac{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2+5^2+6^2}{6}-(3.5)^2=\frac{35}{12}\]
\[\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{35}{12}}\approx1.708\]
