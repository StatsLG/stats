1. Linear Correlation

Quantifying Linear Correlation

Pearson Product-Moment Correlation Coefficient, \(r\) — describes the direction and strength of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables.

It is important to notice that \(r\) only indicates the linear relationship. Another kind of relationship could still be present in the data.

You will not need to calculate \(r\) by hand, but the formula is:

\[r=\frac{SXY}{\sqrt{SSX\,SSY}}\]
\[SSX=\sum(x-\bar{x})^2=\sum x^2-\frac{(\sum x)^2}{n}\]
\[SSY=\sum(y-\bar{y})^2=\sum y^2-\frac{(\sum y)^2}{n}\]
\[SXY=\sum(x-\bar{x})(y-\bar{y})=\sum xy-\frac{(\sum x)(\sum y)}{n}\]

Direction and Strength

Positive \(r\) means larger X values tend to occur with larger Y values. Negative \(r\) means larger values of one variable tend to occur with smaller values of the other.

Strength describes how close the points are to a line.

\[-1\le r\le1\]
  • \(r=1\): perfect positive linear correlation.
  • \(r=-1\): perfect negative linear correlation.
  • \(r=0\): no linear relationship, although another kind of relationship may exist.
  • In this course, a relationship is generally considered strong when \(|r|\ge .75\).

Positive Correlation

Study time and exam grades

Students who study more hours per week tend to have higher exam scores. As X increases, Y tends to increase.

Direction: upward from left to right. Correlation: positive.

Negative Correlation

Temperature and gas bills

For a home heated with natural gas, colder months often have higher gas bills. As temperature increases, the bill tends to decrease.

Remember: positive and negative describe the direction, not how strong the relationship is. Neither one, on its own, proves causation. Both datasets are illustrative classroom examples.

2. Cause and Effect

“Causal” Research — research whose objective is to determine whether one variable causes changes in another.

Correlation Does Not Imply Causation

It is never possible to prove causality just from the relationship between two variables.

Ice Cream and Assaults

The notes give an example of a strong correlation between ice cream consumption and assaults over the months of the year. That does not mean ice cream causes crime. Hot summer temperatures can increase both variables.

Check Your Understanding

A city finds a strong positive correlation between monthly ice cream sales and assaults. Which conclusion is justified from the correlation alone?

Show Explanation

Correlation documents association, not a causal mechanism. Temperature is a plausible rival explanation because warmer months can increase both ice cream consumption and outdoor activity, including opportunities for assaults.

Criteria for Establishing a Causal Relationship

  1. Time order: the cause must occur before the effect.
  2. Co-variation: the graph and correlation coefficient must show a strong relationship.
  3. Rationale: there must be a logical and compelling explanation for the causal mechanism.
  4. Non-spuriousness: rival explanations must be ruled out.

The fourth criterion is especially difficult. Repeating studies under differing conditions can help rule out rival explanations.

1. Time order
The proposed cause occurs first.
2. Co-variation
The variables actually move together.
3. Rationale
A believable mechanism connects them.
4. Non-spuriousness
Competing explanations are ruled out.

3. Linear Regression

Deterministic View — once X is known, the exact value of Y is known.
Regression — a technique used to predict variables based on other variables.
Linear Regression — predicts Y, the response variable, from X, the explanatory variable, using a linear equation.

Prediction is not exact. The error is represented by the vertical distance between a point and the regression line.

\[\hat{Y}=b_0+b_1X\]
  • \(b_0\) is the y-intercept.
  • \(b_1\) is the slope.

Before fitting a line, the scatterplot and \(r\) should indicate that a linear model is reasonable.

The Same Equation You Already Know

In algebra, a straight line is written in slope-intercept form. Linear regression uses the same equation with statistical notation:

\[y=mx+b\qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad\hat{Y}=b_0+b_1X\]
From algebraIn regressionWhat it means
\(x\)\(X\)The known input (explanatory variable), such as dosage.
\(m\)\(b_1\)The slope: predicted change in Y for a one-unit increase in X.
\(b\)\(b_0\)The y-intercept: the model's predicted value when X = 0.
\(y\)\(\hat{Y}\)The predicted response. The hat reminds us it may differ from an actual observation.

Writing the intercept first or last does not change the line: \(b_0+b_1X=b_1X+b_0\). For a prediction, we know X, substitute it into the equation, and calculate \(\hat{Y}\). A regression prediction describes what the fitted line predicts, not what every individual observation must equal.

Method of Least Squares

Method of Least Squares — minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances from the observed points to the regression line.
\[b_1=\frac{SXY}{SSX}\]
\[b_0=\bar{y}-b_1\bar{x}\]

Drug Dosage Regression

\[b_1=.118\qquad b_0=-3.4\]
\[\hat{Y}=-3.4+.118X\]

If \(R^2\) is provided instead of \(r\), take the square root of \(R^2\) and use the sign of the slope to determine whether \(r\) is positive or negative.

How to Make a Prediction

For this illustrative dosage dataset, \(b_1=0.118\) and \(b_0=-3.4\). The slope says that each additional dosage unit corresponds to an increase of 0.118 units in the predicted blood-pressure reduction. The intercept is the line's prediction at dosage 0, but 0 lies outside our observed dosage range, so we should not give it a practical interpretation.

1. Identify X

We want a prediction for dosage 250, so \(X=250\).

2. Substitute

Put 250 in place of X in the regression equation.

\[\hat{Y}=-3.4+0.118(250)\]
3. Calculate

Multiply first: \(0.118(250)=29.5\). Then add the intercept:

\[\hat{Y}=-3.4+29.5=26.1\]
4. Interpret and check

The fitted model predicts a reduction of 26.1 for dosage 250. This is a prediction, not an exact outcome. Since 250 is within the observed dosage range of 100–500, this is interpolation.

Cautions with Regression

Interpolation — predicting Y for X values within the observed range.
Extrapolation — predicting Y for X values beyond the observed range. This should not be done with a basic regression model.

In the drug example, X ranges from 100 to 500. Predicting within that range is reasonable. Predicting at X = 1000 assumes the relationship continues beyond the observed data.

Why Extrapolation Can Go Wrong: Age and Height

Suppose we collect height data from people ranging from birth through age 18 and fit a linear regression model. The model may be useful for summarizing the data we actually observed, but what happens if we keep extending that line into adulthood?

The illustrative data only contain ages 0 through 18, so any prediction above age 18 is extrapolation. The regression equation for these classroom data is approximately:

\[\hat{Y}=25.22+2.61X\]

At age 30, this equation predicts a height of about 103.5 inches—roughly 8 feet 7 inches. The arithmetic is correct, but the prediction is not reasonable because the model assumes people continue growing at the same linear rate after age 18.

Key idea: a regression equation does not know when the real-world relationship changes. Extrapolation asks the model to make predictions in a region where we collected no data.

The height values in this activity are illustrative classroom data, not a medical growth chart.

Extreme Values Can Distort Regression

The least-squares line and \(r\) can be affected greatly by extreme data points.

An influential extreme point can make the remaining observations look compressed and artificially close to a line. Remove an extreme value temporarily and check whether the relationship still exists before trusting the regression.
