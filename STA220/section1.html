The Science of Statistics

How do you define statistics? What do you think of when you hear the term statistics? Can you think of examples where you see statistics utilized?

Statistics are commonly used in most fields of study and are regularly seen in newspapers, on television, and in professional work. Finding examples pertinent to your interests should be quite easy.

Sports

  • Free throw percentage in basketball
  • Quarterback rating in football
  • RBIs in baseball
  • Assists in soccer

Medical

  • Success rates and side effects of a weight-loss drug
  • Percentage of people who go into remission with a type of cancer
  • Effect of a drug on blood pressure

Education

  • Whether writing down material helps comprehension
  • Differences in reading test scores for first-grade males and females
  • Relationship between low academic achievement and depression

Politics

  • Prediction that an incumbent president will be re-elected
  • Senate approval rating
  • Percentage of Kentuckians who support a bill
Statistics — the study of how to collect, organize, analyze, and interpret information.

The advantage of statistics is that it gives us a process for estimation and decision making when faced with uncertainty without prejudice or bias. This ideal will be discussed throughout the semester.

Fundamental Elements of Statistics

To understand the statistical process, we begin with some basic terminology.

Population — the collection of individuals or items of interest.

Examples: all residents of the United States; all admissions to hospitals in Lexington, Kentucky.

Census — measurements are taken from the entire population.

A familiar example is the U.S. Census, conducted every ten years. The Census Bureau attempts to reach every resident, although reaching every individual is difficult.

A census is rarely attempted in ordinary statistical work because it can be expensive and because it is often impossible to get everyone in a population to participate.

Sample — the subset of the population on which we make measurements.
Data — the measurements we collect.
Variable — any characteristic of an individual that can take different values for different individuals.
Statistic — a numerical characteristic of a sample. It is known after the sample is taken, but it can change from sample to sample.
Parameter — a numerical characteristic of a population. It is fixed, but its exact value could only be known from a perfect census.

Statistic vs. Parameter

Because the entire population is often unavailable, we use a statistic from a sample to estimate a parameter of the population.

Example: Kentucky Income

Suppose we want to estimate the average income for all residents of Kentucky. We select 1,000 people from the state and collect their incomes.

Branches of Statistical Applications

Descriptive Statistics

Methods of summarizing a set of data.

There is no sampling error here because you are summarizing the data you actually have.

Inferential Statistics

Methods of making an inference about a population based on information from a sample.

There is error because the entire population is not observed. Probability allows us to calculate and control this error.

Descriptive or Inferential?

  1. A basketball player's free throw percentage based on performance through the midpoint of the season.
  2. The average GPA for all current students based on complete records-office data.
  3. The president's current approval rating based on a group of 5,000 U.S. residents.
  4. A graph showing all sales this year for all major vehicle manufacturers.
  5. Using data from this class to calculate the percentage of students in this class who live in Lexington.
  6. Using data from this class to estimate the percentage of all BCTC students who live in Lexington.
Show Answers
  1. Descriptive — the player's observed performance is being summarized.
  2. Descriptive — complete data for the group of interest are available.
  3. Inferential — a sample of 5,000 residents is used to estimate the population approval rating.
  4. Descriptive — the available sales figures are being summarized.
  5. Descriptive — the class itself is the group of interest.
  6. Inferential — the class is being used as a sample to estimate a characteristic of all BCTC students.

Questions 5 and 6 can use the same data but lead to different analyses. The key is the group of interest.

Types of Mathematical Reasoning

Inductive Reasoning

Used in inferential statistics. It is reasoning by consistency, or bottom-up reasoning: use information from a sample to learn about a population.

Deductive Reasoning

Probability theory. It is reasoning by certainty, or top-down reasoning: begin with a known population and determine properties of samples.

The course proceeds in this order: 1) Descriptive Statistics, 2) Deductive Reasoning (Probability Theory), and 3) Inferential Statistics (Inductive Reasoning).

Data Collection

Bias — a prejudice in one direction. In sampling, this occurs when the sample is selected in a way that favors elements with a particular characteristic.

In inferential statistics, a statistic is used to estimate a parameter. A biased statistic will tend to overestimate or underestimate the value we are trying to predict. Proper sampling techniques can eliminate or at least minimize bias.

A Useful Sample Must Be Representative

For inferential statistics, the sample should resemble the population well enough that results from the sample can reasonably be extended to the population.

Random Sample — a sample determined completely by chance.

Example: A Biased Kentucky Income Sample

Suppose we want to estimate average income for Kentucky residents but sample 1,000 males from Lexington. The notes identify this sample as biased and not representative of the state population.

The goal is for the sample to be similar to the population across relevant characteristics. Random sampling helps accomplish this across many variables at once, although it does not guarantee a perfectly representative sample.

Sample Size and Random Variation

Larger random samples are more likely to resemble the population closely. Think about flipping a fair coin: with more flips, the percentage of tails is more likely to be close to the population value of 50%.

Simple Random Sample (SRS) — a sample of \(n\) measurements selected so that every sample of size \(n\) from the population has an equal probability of being selected.

The notation \(n\) is standard notation for sample size. Everything in this course will be based on a simple random sample.
